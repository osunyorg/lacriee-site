Russell Crotty

body: >-


ink on paper mounted on a fiberglass sphere, diameter 91.4 cm


After studying at the San Francisco Art Institute and the University of California, Russell Crotty now lives in the Santa Monica Mountains in Malibu. There, he has built an observatory on a hilltop overlooking the Pacific Ocean and the city of Los Angeles. Long drawn to drawing and astronomy, Russell Crotty combines these two practices in a body of work that takes the form of large-scale books and sculptures. His compositions blend terrestrial, maritime, and celestial landscapes. They are the result of a process that draws both on the Renaissance concept of “disegno”¹ and on the empirical approaches of 19th-century astronomers. Another distinctive feature is their common yet unconventional use of the ballpoint pen, which defines the expression of their brushwork. Under the California night sky, as he intently observes the movements of the stars and planets, he jots down numerous notes and sketches. Once all these elements are laid out before him, he reinvents, on paper and according to the circumstances and his mood, unique and vibrant landscapes that bear witness to a memory or an unprecedented sensation. Russell Crotty explores various practices that question the concepts of scale and density. In his books, the format he calls FOV (“a field of view”) conveys the limits of a telescope’s field of view through circular drawings. He also creates larger-scale works—a sort of expansive panoramic atlas—depicting a map of the Moon or a hillside relief. This exploration continued in 2000 with a three-dimensional work. Fiberglass globes of varying sizes, covered in paper, represent everything from showers of stars or meteorites to abstract universes. Suspended in space, these spheres serve as miniature models of a galaxy. Russell Crotty offers a condensed interpretation of a universal energy—ever-renewing—that is the source of both life and chaos. The visual artist also incorporates writing into his work. His texts—whether descriptive or allusive, in his books or on his spheres—function as “bad poetry,” playing on both the graphic and semantic qualities of words. They interplay with his drawings like visual poems or prose, reminding us, for example, of our connection to nature. Through his installations, Russell Crotty seems to distance himself from the social and political anxieties that certain artists on the U.S. West Coast have taken on. He prefers the silent observation of boundless spaces—and their reimagining in a dreamlike, phantasmagorical world of his own—to the noisy hustle and bustle of megacities.

Production : La Criée centre d'art contemporain, Rennes Support : Courtesy galerie Suzanne Tarasiève, Paris
