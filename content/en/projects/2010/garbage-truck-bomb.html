frame made of fir wood and particleboard, cell phone, car stereo, amplifier, subwoofer, speakers, touchscreen, cables
1017 x 240 x 362 cm


Garbage Truck Bomb is “an interactive sound installation featuring a booby-trapped garbage truck and its detonator.” The installation is built around the wooden frame of a full-scale garbage truck. This vehicle conceals a cellular GSM detonator that can trigger a violent sonic explosion at any moment. Gas cylinders—typically used in bombings—are repurposed and transformed into sound diffusers. By modeling them out of particleboard, he enhances the resonance of the subwoofers located inside. Here, Damien Marchal subverts the conventions of terrorism to allow us to physically experience the artwork.  Garbage Truck Bomb c enters on the question of taking action and relies on the audience’s decision-making. When faced with the artwork, visitors are compelled to decide where they stand. They have the choice of whether or not to activate the detonation mechanism using their personal cell phone. A digital terminal is provided for this purpose at the entrance. After registering their number, the visitor will receive a message at a random time in the future that will allow them to activate the remote ignition system. Having learned this information, the public may decide to appreciate the installation as a whole or resign themselves to not witnessing the explosion, while reflecting on the significance of the act and its consequences within the exhibition space. This interactive installation prompts us to question the role of the viewer, their relationship to the artwork, and to the exhibition spaces. Without the public’s participation, the artwork remains incomplete. Visitors can therefore choose to remain observers or to pull the trigger, like an activist. By staying out of reach, they position themselves as the mastermind behind an attack, indulging in personal pleasure without regard for potential victims. Each person is thus led to determine their own role. This investment opportunity also reflects our relationship with information—specifically, the information conveyed by the media—and the geopolitical context. Today, car bombs are weapons used worldwide by activist groups that seek to impose their ideology through force and violence. The purpose of such an act of violence is to draw attention to itself. Terrorists are more concerned with the scale of the event and the media coverage of their actions than with their deadly consequences. So, how should we respond to these events? How do we process this information, and how should we view others in this situation? In creating Garbage Truck Bomb , Damien Marchal drew on historical facts detailed in Mike Davis’s book, A Brief History of the Car Bomb (Zone, 2007). The book recounts one of the most significant car bomb attacks, carried out against American troops during the 1983 Lebanese War. The explosion was caused by a dump truck loaded with six metric tons of explosives, which killed hundreds of soldiers at the Beirut airport. This attack had a major geopolitical impact: it forced the U.S. government to back down. The second excerpt outlines the precautionary measures taken by the U.S. Embassy in Kuwait to prohibit vehicles from entering its compound, with the exception of garbage trucks. That’s how the suicide bombers found the loophole… Mike Davis defines the characteristics of the “poor man’s bomber” as the fundamental basis of modern terrorism: “A stealthy, spectacular, inexpensive, easy-to-use, indiscriminately lethal, safe, and anonymous weapon, the car bomb stands out as the ideal weapon for marginal fringe groups, providing them with a striking power disproportionate to their political clout.” An analysis of this historical data and the methods used thus determined the design of the Garbage Truck Bomb.

Production : La Criée centre d'art contemporain, Rennes
