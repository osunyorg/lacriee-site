three elements: water element: a water basin occupying half the space, with floating armchairs food element: a row of tables on trestles; pastries horizontal element: a ceiling that gradually slopes downward, supported by a network of ropes; a floor made of bubble wrap covered with a rug


« As early as 1991, I began working on self-portraits, exploring my body and its dance, my approach, and my choices, in conjunction with a body of work consisting of Polaroids, Super 8 films, and a video choreographic journal. Day by day, I film whatever I encounter that inspires my dance: fragments of landscapes, objects, things, and dances. To perceive the world and translate that perception into spectacular little installations: to improvise within a very precisely composed framework (choice of location, time, clothing, sounds, props, etc.) for the camera. Through this practice—similar to a sketchbook or studies—the creative process continues. A return to oneself in the solitude of the studio, an openness to the geographical, political, and surrounding spaces, and collaborations with other artists. The interplay of compositions and bodily states creates tension. This work is punctuated by spectacular moments. » Catherine Contour, Affiche Catherine Contour , Mont Saint-Martin : association KOB


« What prevails in Catherine Contour’s self-portraits, then, is the quest for what she calls a process of “body-making”: costumes (masks and clothing) to experiment with, visual, textual, and auditory elements to explore, and various materials (cotton, food, etc.) to manipulate. Such combinatory and evolving actions constitute a fundamental first stage of experimentation in which the concept of the self-portrait rejects a simple, figurative, and finished representation of the artist. It is important to note that this moment of action and physical experience emphasizes the unique nature of the performance space. Furthermore, the construction of the body generates movement, matter, and sound, and engages the viewer on a level that goes beyond mere visual observation—unlike self-portraits that are painted, sculpted, or photographed. However, nor is it a “danced self-portrait” in the sense of a predefined stage design and choreography whose form would be purely spectacular. » Larys Frogier, Exhibition Press Release  Catherine Contour, autoportrait à La Criée, 2001
