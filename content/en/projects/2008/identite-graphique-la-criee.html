La Criée’s new visual identity, designed by Lieuxcommuns, supports the art center’s expansion into creative platforms focused on research, international outreach, community engagement, and exhibition practices. The director of the art center, Larys Frogier, repeatedly refers to the philosopher and poet Edouard Glissant when discussing La Criée’s artistic project. The connection between the projects does not lie in the assertion of a single, homogeneous aesthetic sensibility, but rather in a desire to generate the frictions, paradoxes, and contradictions necessary for the richness of artistic projects. This connection can also be found in the demand for political awareness inherent in every artistic, aesthetic, and institutional act. It is this richness arising from the interplay of visual proposals that the graphic design direction champions in order to raise the center’s profile nationally and internationally, as well as to enhance its local visibility and make its new artistic ambitions (as a platform for creation) clear. Lieuxcommuns’ approach explores La Criée’s evolution from the “White Cube”—the famous white exhibition space—into a multifaceted venue with multiple extensions tailored to the art center’s activities and projects. Priority was given to typography as one of the defining graphic elements. Lieuxcommuns conceives of and creates typography as a space for experimentation, just as the art center offers various aesthetic and conceptual forms. Using the Helvetica typeface as an analog framework for the “White Cube,” Lieuxcommuns develops an inventive field of graphic experimentation, much like the initiatives of La Criée, which extend far beyond the gallery’s walls (see LC typefaces). La Criée’s signature is not a single logo but a multifaceted one. Each platform has its own distinct identity. The identity is conceived as a unified whole that incorporates juxtaposition and contrast as a graphic principle. Two typefaces are used simultaneously within the same word: one headlining typeface for the consonants, and the other (Helvetica Ultra Light) for the vowels. This duality highlights both structure and experimentation, as well as fundamentals and forward-thinking. The resulting contrast becomes a defining feature, with otherness serving as a graphic principle. The four signatures and typefaces gave rise to four graphic symbols: cross, circle, triangle, and cube. Serving as visual summaries of each platform, these elements punctuate the graphic spaces, both in stationery and in communication materials. Their simplicity makes them easily recognizable and creates a dialogue with the logos and typefaces to which they refer. The uses of these graphic symbols are defined in this document. This core identity system specific to La Criée is then applied to: -- All the art center’s stationery (letterhead, envelopes, correspondence cards, business cards). -- Information and communication materials: season brochures, cultural outreach materials, press kits, invitation cards. -- Posters in public spaces: these consist of two posters displayed simultaneously in public spaces. One-third of the printed posters feature only a visual and a graphic symbol: the guest artist selects a visual that will serve both as the exhibition’s promotional image and as an extension of the exhibition into public space. This image should therefore be considered in its entirety as a work by the artist. The remaining two-thirds of the printed posters consist exclusively of informational text, utilizing the typography specific to each artistic project. The space devoted to typographic expression in public spaces presents text as much as a source of information as it does as an image. -- La Criée's website

La Criée’s new visual identity, designed by Lieuxcommuns, supports the art center’s expansion into creative platforms focused on research, international outreach, community engagement, and exhibition practices.

The director of the art center, Larys Frogier, repeatedly refers to the philosopher and poet Edouard Glissant when discussing La Criée’s artistic project. The connection between the projects does not lie in the assertion of a single, homogeneous aesthetic sensibility, but rather in a desire to generate the frictions, paradoxes, and contradictions necessary for the richness of artistic projects. This connection can also be found in the demand for political awareness inherent in every artistic, aesthetic, and institutional act.

It is this richness arising from the interplay of visual proposals that the graphic design direction champions in order to raise the center’s profile nationally and internationally, as well as to enhance its local visibility and make its new artistic ambitions (as a platform for creation) clear.
Lieuxcommuns’ approach explores La Criée’s evolution from the “White Cube”—the famous white exhibition space—into a multifaceted venue with multiple extensions tailored to the art center’s activities and projects.

Priority was given to typography as one of the defining graphic elements. Lieuxcommuns conceives of and creates typography as a space for experimentation, just as the art center offers various aesthetic and conceptual forms. Using the Helvetica typeface as an analog framework for the “White Cube,” Lieuxcommuns develops an inventive field of graphic experimentation, much like the initiatives of La Criée, which extend far beyond the gallery’s walls (see LC typefaces).

La Criée’s signature is not a single logo but a multifaceted one. Each platform has its own distinct identity. The identity is conceived as a unified whole that incorporates juxtaposition and contrast as a graphic principle. Two typefaces are used simultaneously within the same word: one headlining typeface for the consonants, and the other (Helvetica Ultra Light) for the vowels. This duality highlights both structure and experimentation, as well as fundamentals and forward-thinking. The resulting contrast becomes a defining feature, with otherness serving as a graphic principle.

The four signatures and typefaces gave rise to four graphic symbols: cross, circle, triangle, and cube. Serving as visual summaries of each platform, these elements punctuate the graphic spaces, both in stationery and in communication materials. Their simplicity makes them easily recognizable and creates a dialogue with the logos and typefaces to which they refer. The uses of these graphic symbols are defined in this document.

This core identity system specific to La Criée is then applied to:

-- All the art center’s stationery (letterhead, envelopes, correspondence cards, business cards).
-- Information and communication materials: season brochures, cultural outreach materials, press kits, invitation cards.
-- Posters in public spaces: these consist of two posters displayed simultaneously in public spaces. One-third of the printed posters feature only a visual and a graphic symbol: the guest artist selects a visual that will serve both as the exhibition’s promotional image and as an extension of the exhibition into public space. This image should therefore be considered in its entirety as a work by the artist. The remaining two-thirds of the printed posters consist exclusively of informational text, utilizing the typography specific to each artistic project. The space devoted to typographic expression in public spaces presents text as much as a source of information as it does as an image.
-- La Criée's website
